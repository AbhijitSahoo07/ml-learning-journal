# Hierarchical Planning

## Overview
Hierarchical Planning is a powerful approach in artificial intelligence and machine learning, particularly in reinforcement learning and automated planning, that tackles complex problems by breaking them down into a hierarchy of simpler, more manageable subproblems. Imagine you want to build a house. You wouldn't start by thinking about placing individual bricks. Instead, you'd first plan at a high level: "design the house," then "lay the foundation," "build the walls," "install the roof," and so on. Each of these high-level tasks can then be further broken down into more detailed subtasks.

This is precisely what Hierarchical Planning does. It operates on multiple levels of abstraction. A "high-level" planner deals with abstract goals and actions, making broad decisions. These abstract actions are then refined by "low-level" planners into concrete, primitive actions that can be executed in the environment. This structured approach allows intelligent agents to reason about long-term goals and complex sequences of actions more efficiently than traditional "flat" planning methods.

## What Problem It Solves
Hierarchical Planning primarily addresses several critical challenges faced by traditional, "flat" planning systems:

1.  **Complexity and Scalability (Curse of Dimensionality):** In many real-world scenarios, the state space (all possible configurations of the environment) and action space (all possible actions an agent can take) can be astronomically large. Traditional planners often struggle with this "curse of dimensionality," where the computational cost grows exponentially with the number of states and actions. Hierarchical Planning reduces this complexity by operating on abstract representations, effectively shrinking the problem space at higher levels.

2.  **Long-Horizon Planning:** When an agent needs to achieve a goal that requires a very long sequence of primitive actions, flat planners can become inefficient or even infeasible. They might get lost in local optima or take too long to explore the vast search space. Hierarchical Planning allows the agent to plan over longer horizons by abstracting away low-level details, focusing on key milestones or subgoals.

3.  **Lack of Interpretability:** A long sequence of primitive actions generated by a flat planner can be difficult for humans to understand or debug. Hierarchical plans, by their nature, provide a structured breakdown of the solution, making the agent's reasoning process more transparent and interpretable.

4.  **Transfer Learning and Reusability:** Once a high-level skill or abstract action (e.g., "navigate to room X") is learned, it can often be reused in different contexts or for different high-level goals without needing to re-learn all the primitive actions from scratch. This promotes modularity and reusability of learned behaviors.

5.  **Handling Uncertainty and Dynamic Environments:** By abstracting away details, hierarchical planners can be more robust to minor perturbations or uncertainties in the environment. High-level plans can remain stable even if low-level execution needs to adapt to local changes.

## How It Works
Hierarchical Planning typically involves a multi-layered architecture, often with at least two levels: a high-level planner and one or more low-level planners.

Here's a step-by-step breakdown of how it generally works:

1.  **Problem Decomposition:**
    *   The initial complex goal is received by the **High-Level Planner**.
    *   The high-level planner doesn't deal with primitive actions (like "move forward 1 meter"). Instead, it operates on **abstract states** and **abstract actions**. Abstract states might represent locations (e.g., "in the kitchen," "at the charging station") or conditions (e.g., "door is open"). Abstract actions are skills or subroutines (e.g., "go to the kitchen," "open the door," "charge battery").
    *   The high-level planner's task is to find a sequence of abstract actions that transforms the current abstract state into the desired abstract goal state. This sequence forms a **high-level plan**.

2.  **Abstract Planning:**
    *   Using its simplified model of the world (which ignores many low-level details), the high-level planner searches for a path through the abstract state space. This search is much faster because the state and action spaces are significantly smaller.
    *   The output is a sequence of abstract actions: $A_{abstract\_1}, A_{abstract\_2}, ..., A_{abstract\_k}$.

3.  **Plan Refinement (Delegation to Low-Level Planners):**
    *   Each abstract action in the high-level plan is then passed down to a **Low-Level Planner**.
    *   A low-level planner takes an abstract action (which acts as its specific subgoal) and the current concrete state of the environment.
    *   Its job is to find a sequence of **primitive actions** (e.g., "move North," "turn 90 degrees left," "grasp object") that achieves that specific abstract action's subgoal.
    *   The low-level planner operates on the full, detailed state space and uses its knowledge of primitive actions and immediate environmental dynamics.

4.  **Execution and Monitoring:**
    *   The sequence of primitive actions generated by the low-level planner is then executed in the real or simulated environment.
    *   During execution, the system monitors the environment to ensure the low-level plan is progressing as expected.
    *   Once a low-level plan (corresponding to one abstract action) is completed, the high-level planner updates its current abstract state and proceeds to refine the next abstract action in its high-level plan.

5.  **Feedback and Replanning:**
    *   If a low-level plan fails (e.g., the robot gets stuck, or a door doesn't open), or if the environment changes unexpectedly, feedback is sent back up the hierarchy.
    *   The high-level planner might then need to replan from its current abstract state, potentially generating a new sequence of abstract actions to achieve the overall goal. This allows for robustness and adaptability.

This iterative process of high-level decision-making and low-level execution continues until the ultimate goal is achieved.

## Mathematical Intuition

Let's formalize the concept using the framework of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), which are commonly used in planning and reinforcement learning.

A standard MDP is defined by a tuple $(S, A, T, R, \gamma)$:
*   $S$: A set of states.
*   $A$: A set of actions.
*   $T(s' | s, a)$: A transition function, giving the probability of reaching state $s'$ from state $s$ after taking action $a$.
*   $R(s, a, s')$: A reward function, giving the immediate reward for transitioning from $s$ to $s'$ via action $a$.
*   $\gamma$: A discount factor.

The goal in an MDP is to find a policy $\pi: S \rightarrow A$ that maximizes the expected cumulative reward.

In Hierarchical Planning, we introduce the concept of **abstract states** and **abstract actions**.

1.  **Abstract States:**
    Instead of the full, detailed state space $S$, we define a set of abstract states $S_A$. Each abstract state $s_A \in S_A$ corresponds to a subset of primitive states $S_{s_A} \subseteq S$.
    For example, if $S$ includes exact $(x, y)$ coordinates, $S_A$ might include "Room 1", "Room 2", where "Room 1" corresponds to all $(x, y)$ coordinates within a certain boundary.
    We can define a mapping $\phi: S \rightarrow S_A$ that maps a primitive state to its corresponding abstract state.

2.  **Abstract Actions (Options/Skills):**
    An abstract action, often called an **option** or **skill** in reinforcement learning literature (Sutton, Precup, Singh, 1999), is a temporally extended course of action. An option $o$ is a tuple $(\mathcal{I}, \pi_o, \beta_o)$:
    *   $\mathcal{I} \subseteq S$: The set of states in which the option can be initiated.
    *   $\pi_o: S \rightarrow A$: The policy followed while the option is executing. This policy maps primitive states to primitive actions.
    *   $\beta_o: S \rightarrow [0, 1]$: The termination condition, giving the probability that the option terminates in state $s$.

    When an option $o$ is chosen, its internal policy $\pi_o$ is executed until the option terminates according to $\beta_o$.

3.  **High-Level MDP:**
    We can then define a **High-Level MDP** (or Abstract MDP) using these abstract concepts: $(S_A, O, T_A, R_A, \gamma_A)$.
    *   $S_A$: The set of abstract states.
    *   $O$: The set of available options (abstract actions).
    *   $T_A(s'_A | s_A, o)$: The transition function for abstract states. This is more complex than primitive transitions. If we start in an abstract state $s_A$ (meaning we are in some primitive state $s \in S_{s_A}$) and execute option $o$, what is the probability of ending up in abstract state $s'_A$?
        This can be calculated by considering all possible primitive states $s \in S_{s_A}$, executing option $o$ from $s$ until it terminates in some $s''$, and then mapping $s''$ to its abstract state $\phi(s'')$.
        $$T_A(s'_A | s_A, o) = \sum_{s \in S_{s_A}} P(s | s_A) \sum_{s'' \in S_{s'_A}} P(s'' \text{ terminates } o | s)$$
        where $P(s | s_A)$ is the probability of being in primitive state $s$ given abstract state $s_A$, and $P(s'' \text{ terminates } o | s)$ is the probability that option $o$ initiated in $s$ terminates in $s''$.

    *   $R_A(s_A, o, s'_A)$: The reward function for abstract transitions. This is typically the sum of discounted primitive rewards accumulated while executing option $o$ from $s_A$ until it terminates in $s'_A$.
        $$R_A(s_A, o, s'_A) = E \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{k-1} \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) \mid s_0 \in S_{s_A}, \text{option } o \text{ executed, terminates at } s_k \in S_{s'_A} \right]$$
        where $k$ is the duration of the option.

    *   $\gamma_A$: A potentially adjusted discount factor for the high-level MDP.

The high-level planner then solves this Abstract MDP to find an optimal policy $\pi_A: S_A \rightarrow O$ that selects the best option to execute from any given abstract state. When an option $o$ is selected, its internal policy $\pi_o$ (which is a low-level policy) takes over to execute primitive actions.

This mathematical framework shows how Hierarchical Planning effectively transforms a single, large MDP into a smaller, abstract MDP at the higher level, where each "action" in the abstract MDP corresponds to solving a sub-MDP (or executing a policy) at the lower level. This decomposition significantly reduces the effective planning horizon and the complexity of the search space.

## Advantages
*   **Reduced Complexity:** By abstracting away details, it significantly reduces the state and action space at higher levels, making planning computationally more tractable for complex problems.
*   **Improved Scalability:** Can handle problems with very large state spaces and long planning horizons that would be infeasible for flat planners.
*   **Enhanced Interpretability:** The hierarchical structure provides a clear breakdown of the agent's intentions and actions, making the planning process easier to understand, debug, and explain to humans.
*   **Faster Learning/Planning:** The smaller search space at higher levels often leads to quicker convergence in learning algorithms or faster plan generation.
*   **Modularity and Reusability:** Learned skills or abstract actions can be reused across different tasks or parts of the environment, promoting efficient transfer learning.
*   **Robustness to Uncertainty:** High-level plans can be more stable and less sensitive to minor environmental changes, as low-level planners can adapt to local variations.
*   **Natural Problem Representation:** Many real-world problems naturally lend themselves to hierarchical decomposition, mirroring how humans approach complex tasks.

## Disadvantages
*   **Suboptimality:** Decomposing a problem into subproblems and solving them independently can lead to suboptimal overall solutions. The optimal high-level plan might not be composed of individually optimal low-level plans.
*   **Difficulty in Defining Hierarchy:** Designing an effective hierarchy (i.e., defining appropriate abstract states, actions, and subgoals) can be challenging and often requires significant domain knowledge or careful engineering. Poorly designed hierarchies can hinder performance.
*   **Deadlocks and Infeasibility:** If the high-level planner makes a decision that leads to an abstract state from which no low-level plan can achieve the next abstract goal, the system can get stuck or fail.
*   **Increased Overhead:** Managing multiple planners and coordinating between different levels of abstraction introduces additional computational and design overhead.
*   **Communication Challenges:** Effective communication and goal passing between different levels of the hierarchy are crucial. Inefficient communication can lead to inconsistencies or failures.
*   **Lack of Generalization:** While skills can be reused, the specific hierarchy itself might not generalize well to drastically different environments or tasks, requiring re-engineering.

## Real World Applications
1.  **Robotics (Navigation and Manipulation):**
    *   **Navigation:** A robot needs to deliver a package across a building. A high-level planner might decide on a sequence of rooms to traverse ("Go to Room A, then Room B, then Room C"). A low-level planner for each room would then handle obstacle avoidance, pathfinding within the room, and door opening using primitive motor commands.
    *   **Manipulation:** For assembling a product, a high-level plan might be "Pick up Part A," "Attach Part A to Part B," "Tighten Screws." Each of these abstract actions would be broken down by low-level planners into precise gripper movements, force control, and vision-guided adjustments.

2.  **Autonomous Driving:**
    *   **Route Planning:** A high-level planner determines the overall route from origin to destination, considering traffic, road types, and user preferences (e.g., "Take highway," "Avoid tolls").
    *   **Maneuver Planning:** An intermediate-level planner decides on maneuvers like "Change lane," "Overtake," "Turn left at intersection."
    *   **Vehicle Control:** A low-level planner translates these maneuvers into specific steering, acceleration, and braking commands, reacting to immediate sensor data and maintaining safety.

3.  **Game AI:**
    *   **Character Behavior:** In complex games, AI characters use hierarchical planning. A high-level goal might be "Defeat the enemy base." This breaks down into "Gather resources," "Build units," "Attack enemy." "Attack enemy" then breaks down into "Move units to front line," "Target weakest enemy," "Use special ability," which are executed by low-level tactical AI.
    *   **NPC Interaction:** NPCs might have high-level goals like "Patrol area," "Interact with player," "Rest." Low-level planners handle the specific pathfinding, dialogue choices, and animation sequences.

4.  **Logistics and Supply Chain Management:**
    *   **Strategic Planning:** High-level decisions involve warehouse placement, inventory levels, and transportation network design.
    *   **Tactical Planning:** Mid-level planners optimize vehicle routing, scheduling deliveries, and assigning tasks to workers.
    *   **Operational Planning:** Low-level planners manage real-time dispatching, dynamic rerouting due to traffic, and individual package handling.

5.  **Manufacturing and Process Control:**
    *   **Production Scheduling:** A high-level planner might schedule the production of different product batches over weeks or months.
    *   **Machine Operation:** Low-level planners control individual machines, optimizing parameters like temperature, pressure, and speed for specific tasks, ensuring quality and efficiency.

## Python Example

As Hierarchical Planning is an architectural approach rather than a specific algorithm implemented in a library, we'll create a simplified simulation of a robot navigating a multi-room environment.

```python
import numpy as np
import collections

# --- Environment Definition ---
class GridWorld:
    def __init__(self, layout, rooms, doors):
        self.layout = np.array([list(row) for row in layout])
        self.height, self.width = self.layout.shape
        self.rooms = rooms  # {'RoomA': [(r1,c1), (r2,c2)], ...}
        self.doors = doors  # {'Door1': (r,c), 'Door2': (r,c), ...}
        self.door_states = {door_name: False for door_name in self.doors} # False: closed, True: open

        # Map coordinates to room names for quick lookup
        self.coord_to_room = {}
        for room_name, coords_list in self.rooms.items():
            for r, c in coords_list:
                self.coord_to_room[(r, c)] = room_name

    def is_valid(self, r, c):
        return 0 <= r < self.height and 0 <= c < self.width and self.layout[r, c] != '#'

    def get_room_at_coord(self, r, c):
        return self.coord_to_room.get((r, c), None)

    def get_door_name_at_coord(self, r, c):
        for name, coord in self.doors.items():
            if coord == (r, c):
                return name
        return None

    def open_door(self, door_name):
        if door_name in self.door_states:
            self.door_states[door_name] = True
            print(f"  [ENV] Door '{door_name}' is now OPEN.")
            return True
        return False

    def is_door_open(self, door_name):
        return self.door_states.get(door_name, False)

    def get_neighbors(self, r, c):
        neighbors = []
        for dr, dc in [(-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)]: # Up, Down, Left, Right
            nr, nc = r + dr, c + dc
            if self.is_valid(nr, nc):
                # Check if it's a closed door
                door_name = self.get_door_name_at_coord(nr, nc)
                if door_name and not self.is_door_open(door_name):
                    continue # Cannot move through a closed door
                neighbors.append((nr, nc))
        return neighbors

# --- Low-Level Planner (Pathfinding within a room or to a door) ---
class LowLevelPlanner:
    def __init__(self, env):
        self.env = env

    def find_path(self, start_pos, target_pos, current_door_states):
        """
        Finds a path from start_pos to target_pos using BFS.
        Considers current door states.
        Returns a list of (r, c) coordinates representing the path.
        """
        queue = collections.deque([(start_pos, [start_pos])])
        visited = {start_pos}

        # Temporarily update environment door states for pathfinding
        original_door_states = self.env.door_states.copy()
        self.env.door_states = current_door_states

        while queue:
            current_coord, path = queue.popleft()

            if current_coord == target_pos:
                self.env.door_states = original_door_states # Restore original states
                return path

            for neighbor in self.env.get_neighbors(*current_coord):
                if neighbor not in visited:
                    visited.add(neighbor)
                    queue.append((neighbor, path + [neighbor]))
        
        self.env.door_states = original_door_states # Restore original states
        return None # No path found

    def execute_abstract_action(self, robot_pos, abstract_action_goal, current_door_states):
        """
        Executes an abstract action (e.g., 'GO_TO_ROOM', 'OPEN_DOOR').
        Returns the new robot position and a list of primitive actions.
        """
        primitive_actions = []
        new_robot_pos = robot_pos

        if abstract_action_goal.startswith("GO_TO_ROOM_"):
            target_room_name = abstract_action_goal.split("_")[3]
            print(f"  [LLP] Planning to go to room: {target_room_name}")
            
            # Find a coordinate within the target room that is reachable
            target_coords_in_room = self.env.rooms.get(target_room_name, [])
            
            # For simplicity, just pick the first coordinate in the room as a target
            # A more sophisticated approach would find the closest reachable point
            if not target_coords_in_room:
                print(f"  [LLP] Error: Room '{target_room_name}' not found or empty.")
                return robot_pos, []

            target_coord = target_coords_in_room[0] # Just pick one point in the room

            path = self.find_path(robot_pos, target_coord, current_door_states)
            if path:
                for i in range(len(path) - 1):
                    r1, c1 = path[i]
                    r2, c2 = path[i+1]
                    if r2 > r1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_DOWN")
                    elif r2 < r1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_UP")
                    elif c2 > c1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_RIGHT")
                    elif c2 < c1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_LEFT")
                new_robot_pos = path[-1]
                print(f"  [LLP] Path found to {target_room_name}: {primitive_actions}")
            else:
                print(f"  [LLP] No path found to {target_room_name} from {robot_pos}.")
                return robot_pos, []

        elif abstract_action_goal.startswith("OPEN_DOOR_"):
            door_name = abstract_action_goal.split("_")[2]
            print(f"  [LLP] Planning to open door: {door_name}")
            
            door_coord = self.env.doors.get(door_name)
            if not door_coord:
                print(f"  [LLP] Error: Door '{door_name}' not found.")
                return robot_pos, []

            # First, move to the door's location (or adjacent to it)
            path_to_door = self.find_path(robot_pos, door_coord, current_door_states)
            if path_to_door:
                for i in range(len(path_to_door) - 1):
                    r1, c1 = path_to_door[i]
                    r2, c2 = path_to_door[i+1]
                    if r2 > r1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_DOWN")
                    elif r2 < r1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_UP")
                    elif c2 > c1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_RIGHT")
                    elif c2 < c1: primitive_actions.append("MOVE_LEFT")
                new_robot_pos = path_to_door[-1]
                primitive_actions.append(f"ACTUATE_DOOR_{door_name}") # Primitive action to open
                print(f"  [LLP] Path to door {door_name} found, and actuation planned.")
            else:
                print(f"  [LLP] No path found to door {door_name} from {robot_pos}.")
                return robot_pos, []

        else:
            print(f"  [LLP] Unknown abstract action: {abstract_action_goal}")
            return robot_pos, []
        
        return new_robot_pos, primitive_actions

# --- High-Level Planner (Room-to-room navigation) ---
class HighLevelPlanner:
    def __init__(self, env):
        self.env = env
        self.room_graph = self._build_room_graph()

    def _build_room_graph(self):
        """
        Builds a graph where nodes are rooms and edges are doors connecting them.
        """
        graph = collections.defaultdict(list)
        for door_name, door_coord in self.env.doors.items():
            # Find rooms adjacent to this door
            adjacent_rooms = set()
            for dr, dc in [(-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)]:
                nr, nc = door_coord[0] + dr, door_coord[1] + dc
                room_name = self.env.get_room_at_coord(nr, nc)
                if room_name:
                    adjacent_rooms.add(room_name)
            
            # If a door connects two rooms, add an edge
            if len(adjacent_rooms) == 2:
                room1, room2 = list(adjacent_rooms)
                graph[room1].append((room2, door_name))
                graph[room2].append((room1, door_name))
            elif len(adjacent_rooms) == 1: # Door might lead to an open space or outside
                 room1 = list(adjacent_rooms)[0]
                 graph[room1].append((f"OUTSIDE_{door_name}", door_name)) # Placeholder for outside
        return graph

    def plan_abstract_actions(self, start_room, target_room, current_door_states):
        """
        Finds a sequence of abstract actions (GO_TO_ROOM, OPEN_DOOR)
        to get from start_room to target_room. Uses BFS on the room graph.
        """
        print(f"[HLP] Planning from {start_room} to {target_room}")
        queue = collections.deque([(start_room, [])]) # (current_room, abstract_plan)
        visited = {start_room}

        while queue:
            current_room, current_plan = queue.popleft()

            if current_room == target_room:
                return current_plan

            for neighbor_room, door_name in self.room_graph[current_room]:
                if neighbor_room not in visited:
                    visited.add(neighbor_room)
                    
                    new_plan = list(current_plan) # Copy the plan
                    
                    # If the door is closed, add an action to open it
                    if not current_door_states.get(door_name, False):
                        new_plan.append(f"OPEN_DOOR_{door_name}")
                    
                    new_plan.append(f"GO_TO_ROOM_{neighbor_room}")
                    queue.append((neighbor_room, new_plan))
        
        return None # No abstract plan found

# --- Hierarchical Planner Orchestrator ---
class HierarchicalPlanner:
    def __init__(self, env, initial_robot_pos):
        self.env = env
        self.robot_pos = initial_robot_pos
        self.high_level_planner = HighLevelPlanner(env)
        self.low_level_planner = LowLevelPlanner(env)
        self.current_room = env.get_room_at_coord(*initial_robot_pos)
        print(f"Robot starts at {self.robot_pos} in {self.current_room}")

    def plan_and_execute(self, final_target_room):
        print(f"\n--- Starting Hierarchical Planning to reach {final_target_room} ---")
        
        while self.current_room != final_target_room:
            print(f"\n[HP] Current room: {self.current_room}. Target: {final_target_room}")
            
            # 1. High-Level Planning
            abstract_plan = self.high_level_planner.plan_abstract_actions(
                self.current_room, final_target_room, self.env.door_states
            )

            if not abstract_plan:
                print("[HP] No high-level plan found. Aborting.")
                return False

            print(f"[HP] High-level plan: {abstract_plan}")

            # 2. Execute Abstract Actions sequentially
            for abstract_action in abstract_plan:
                print(f"\n[HP] Executing abstract action: {abstract_action}")
                
                if abstract_action.startswith("OPEN_DOOR_"):
                    door_name = abstract_action.split("_")[2]
                    # Low-level plan to move to door and open it
                    new_pos, primitive_actions = self.low_level_planner.execute_abstract_action(
                        self.robot_pos, abstract_action, self.env.door_states
                    )
                    if not primitive_actions:
                        print(f"[HP] Failed to execute {abstract_action}. Replanning needed.")
                        break # Break from inner loop to trigger high-level replan
                    
                    self.robot_pos = new_pos
                    self.env.open_door(door_name) # Update environment state
                    print(f"  [HP] Robot is now at {self.robot_pos}. Door '{door_name}' is open.")

                elif abstract_action.startswith("GO_TO_ROOM_"):
                    target_room_name = abstract_action.split("_")[3]
                    # Low-level plan to move to the target room
                    new_pos, primitive_actions = self.low_level_planner.execute_abstract_action(
                        self.robot_pos, abstract_action, self.env.door_states
                    )
                    if not primitive_actions:
                        print(f"[HP] Failed to execute {abstract_action}. Replanning needed.")
                        break # Break from inner loop to trigger high-level replan
                    
                    self.robot_pos = new_pos
                    self.current_room = self.env.get_room_at_coord(*self.robot_pos)
                    print(f"  [HP] Robot is now at {self.robot_pos} in {self.current_room}.")
                    
                    if self.current_room == final_target_room:
                        print(f"[HP] Reached final target room: {final_target_room}!")
                        return True
                
                # If the current room changed, and it's not the final target,
                # the high-level planner needs to re-evaluate.
                # This simple example assumes high-level plan is executed fully,
                # but in dynamic environments, you'd check after each primitive action.
                if self.current_room != self.env.get_room_at_coord(*self.robot_pos):
                    self.current_room = self.env.get_room_at_coord(*self.robot_pos)
                    print(f"[HP] Robot moved to a new room: {self.current_room}. Re-evaluating high-level plan.")
                    break # Break from inner loop to trigger high-level replan

            else: # This 'else' block executes if the for loop completes without a 'break'
                if self.current_room == final_target_room:
                    print(f"[HP] Successfully reached final target room: {final_target_room}!")
                    return True
        
        print(f"[HP] Final position: {self.robot_pos} in {self.current_room}")
        return self.current_room == final_target_room

    doors = {
        'Door1': (1, 3), # Connects RoomA to RoomC (conceptually, via a corridor)
        'Door2': (3, 3)  # Connects RoomB to RoomC
    }

    env = GridWorld(grid_layout, rooms, doors)

    # Initial robot position
    initial_robot_pos = (1, 1) # Start in RoomA

    # Create and run the hierarchical planner
    planner = HierarchicalPlanner(env, initial_robot_pos)
    
    # Try to reach RoomC
    target_room = 'RoomC'
    success = planner.plan_and_execute(target_room)

    print(f"\n--- Planning Result ---")
    print(f"Target Room: {target_room}")
    print(f"Reached: {success}")
    print(f"Final Robot Position: {planner.robot_pos}")
    print(f"Final Robot Room: {planner.current_room}")
    print(f"Final Door States: {env.door_states}")

    print("\n--- Another Scenario: Try to reach RoomB from RoomC ---")
    # Reset environment and robot for a new scenario
    env = GridWorld(grid_layout, rooms, doors) # Re-initialize to close doors
    initial_robot_pos_2 = (3, 3) # Start in RoomC
    planner_2 = HierarchicalPlanner(env, initial_robot_pos_2)
    target_room_2 = 'RoomB'
    success_2 = planner_2.plan_and_execute(target_room_2)

    print(f"\n--- Planning Result ---")
    print(f"Target Room: {target_room_2}")
    print(f"Reached: {success_2}")
    print(f"Final Robot Position: {planner_2.robot_pos}")
    print(f"Final Robot Room: {planner_2.current_room}")
    print(f"Final Door States: {env.door_states}")
```

**Explanation of the Python Example:**

1.  **`GridWorld` (Environment):**
    *   Represents a simple grid-based world with walls (`#`), floor (`.`), rooms, and doors.
    *   Keeps track of which doors are open or closed.
    *   `get_neighbors` method ensures the robot cannot move through walls or closed doors.

2.  **`LowLevelPlanner`:**
    *   Responsible for executing **abstract actions** by breaking them down into **primitive actions**.
    *   `find_path`: Uses Breadth-First Search (BFS) to find a sequence of grid coordinates (a path) between two points, respecting walls and current door states.
    *   `execute_abstract_action`: Takes an abstract goal (e.g., `"GO_TO_ROOM_RoomC"`, `"OPEN_DOOR_Door1"`) and generates a list of primitive moves (`"MOVE_UP"`, `"MOVE_RIGHT"`, `"ACTUATE_DOOR_Door1"`). It uses `find_path` for movement.

3.  **`HighLevelPlanner`:**
    *   Responsible for making **abstract decisions** based on **abstract states** (rooms).
    *   `_build_room_graph`: Creates a graph where nodes are rooms and edges represent connections via doors. This is the abstract representation of the environment.
    *   `plan_abstract_actions`: Uses BFS on this room graph to find a sequence of abstract actions (e.g., `["OPEN_DOOR_Door1", "GO_TO_ROOM_RoomC"]`) to get from a starting room to a target room. It considers if doors need to be opened.

4.  **`HierarchicalPlanner` (Orchestrator):**
    *   This is the main class that orchestrates the high-level and low-level planners.
    *   It maintains the robot's current position and room.
    *   `plan_and_execute`:
        *   Continuously checks if the robot is in the `final_target_room`.
        *   If not, it calls the `HighLevelPlanner` to get an `abstract_plan`.
        *   It then iterates through each `abstract_action` in the `abstract_plan`.
        *   For each `abstract_action`, it calls the `LowLevelPlanner` to get the `primitive_actions` and execute them.
        *   Crucially, after executing an abstract action (like opening a door or moving to a new room), it updates the environment state and the robot's current room. If the robot ends up in a different room than expected, or if a low-level plan fails, it breaks the current abstract plan execution and triggers a new high-level plan, demonstrating replanning.

This example clearly shows the decomposition of a complex task (reaching a target room) into simpler, abstract subgoals (go to an intermediate room, open a door), which are then further broken down into primitive actions (move, actuate).

## Interview Questions

1.  **What is Hierarchical Planning, and why is it used in AI?**
    *   **Answer:** Hierarchical Planning is an AI approach that breaks down complex, long-horizon problems into a series of simpler, more manageable subproblems arranged in a hierarchy. It's used to address the challenges of scalability, computational complexity (curse of dimensionality), and long-term reasoning that traditional "flat" planning methods face in large state and action spaces. It allows agents to plan at multiple levels of abstraction.

2.  **Explain the core components of a Hierarchical Planning system.**
    *   **Answer:** The core components typically include:
        *   **High-Level Planner:** Operates on abstract states and abstract actions (skills/options). It generates a sequence of abstract goals or subtasks.
        *   **Low-Level Planner(s):** Takes an abstract action from the high-level planner as a subgoal and refines it into a sequence of primitive actions that can be executed in the environment. It operates on the detailed state space.
        *   **Abstract States:** Simplified representations of the environment, grouping multiple primitive states (e.g., "in Room A" instead of specific (x,y) coordinates).
        *   **Abstract Actions (Options/Skills):** Temporally extended actions or subroutines that achieve a specific subgoal (e.g., "go to the door," "open the door").
        *   **Environment Model:** Detailed for low-level, abstract for high-level.

3.  **How does Hierarchical Planning help mitigate the "curse of dimensionality"?**
    *   **Answer:** The curse of dimensionality arises when the state and action spaces grow exponentially, making exhaustive search or learning infeasible. Hierarchical Planning mitigates this by:
        *   **Reducing State Space:** At the high level, it operates on abstract states, which are aggregates of many primitive states, effectively shrinking the state space.
        *   **Reducing Action Space:** Abstract actions (options/skills) represent sequences of primitive actions. This reduces the effective branching factor at the high level, as a single abstract action replaces many primitive choices.
        *   This allows the high-level planner to reason over longer horizons more efficiently.

4.  **What are "options" in the context of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning, and how do they relate to Hierarchical Planning?**
    *   **Answer:** "Options" (as introduced by Sutton, Precup, and Singh) are a formal mathematical framework for defining abstract actions or skills in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning. An option is a temporally extended action defined by an initiation set (where it can start), a policy (how it acts), and a termination condition (when it stops). They directly relate to Hierarchical Planning by providing a concrete way to represent the "abstract actions" that a high-level planner can choose, allowing the high-level planner to operate over a larger time scale.

5.  **Discuss the trade-off between optimality and computational efficiency in Hierarchical Planning.**
    *   **Answer:** Hierarchical Planning generally sacrifices global optimality for computational efficiency. By decomposing the problem and planning at abstract levels, it significantly speeds up the planning process. However, because the high-level planner makes decisions based on a simplified model and low-level planners optimize for subgoals, the resulting overall plan might not be the absolute optimal sequence of primitive actions. There's a risk of local optima or suboptimal paths being chosen at the abstract level that might not be globally optimal.

6.  **What are some challenges in designing an effective hierarchical planning system?**
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Defining the Hierarchy:** Determining the right levels of abstraction, what constitutes an abstract state, and how to define useful abstract actions/skills is often non-trivial and requires significant domain expertise.
        *   **Goal Specification:** Clearly defining subgoals for low-level planners that align with the high-level objectives.
        *   **Communication and Coordination:** Ensuring seamless information flow and consistent goal interpretation between different levels.
        *   **Suboptimality:** Mitigating the risk of generating suboptimal plans.
        *   **Learning Hierarchies:** Automatically learning effective hierarchies from data is an active research area and remains challenging.

7.  **How does Hierarchical Planning differ from traditional "flat" planning?**
    *   **Answer:**
        *   **Abstraction Levels:** Flat planning operates on a single level of detail (primitive states and actions), while hierarchical planning uses multiple levels of abstraction.
        *   **Complexity:** Flat planning struggles with large state/action spaces and long horizons; hierarchical planning manages this by reducing effective complexity.
        *   **Interpretability:** Hierarchical plans are generally more interpretable due to their structured nature.
        *   **Efficiency:** Hierarchical planning is often more computationally efficient for complex tasks, though potentially suboptimal.

8.  **Can Hierarchical Planning handle dynamic environments or unexpected events? How?**
    *   **Answer:** Yes, it can. When an unexpected event occurs or a low-level plan fails, the low-level planner can report this failure up to the high-level planner. The high-level planner can then re-evaluate its current abstract state and replan from that point, potentially generating a new sequence of abstract actions to adapt to the changed environment. This makes it robust to local uncertainties, as the high-level plan might remain valid even if low-level execution needs to change.

9.  **Provide an example of Hierarchical Planning in a real-world scenario, clearly identifying the high-level and low-level components.**
    *   **Answer:** **Autonomous Driving:**
        *   **High-Level Planner:** Decides the overall route from city A to city B (e.g., "Take Highway 101, then exit at Main Street"). Abstract states are locations (cities, highways, major roads), abstract actions are major route segments.
        *   **Mid-Level Planner:** For a segment like "Take Highway 101," it plans maneuvers like "Merge onto highway," "Change lanes to overtake," "Take exit." Abstract states are current lane, speed, proximity to other cars; abstract actions are specific driving maneuvers.
        *   **Low-Level Planner:** For a maneuver like "Change lane," it translates this into primitive actions: "Adjust steering angle by X degrees," "Increase throttle by Y%," "Apply Z braking force." Primitive states are sensor readings (camera, lidar, radar); primitive actions are direct vehicle controls.

10. **What are the potential benefits of combining Hierarchical Planning with Reinforcement Learning?**
    *   **Answer:** This combination, known as Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), offers significant benefits:
        *   **Faster Learning:** By learning and reusing skills (options), HRL agents can learn complex tasks much faster than flat RL agents, as they don't have to learn primitive actions from scratch for every new subtask.
        *   **Improved Exploration:** Options allow for more directed exploration, as the agent can explore at a higher level of abstraction.
        *   **Better Generalization:** Learned skills can often be generalized and reused in different contexts or for different high-level goals.
        *   **Handling Sparse Rewards:** Options can help bridge long time horizons, making it easier to learn in environments with sparse rewards, as options can provide intrinsic rewards for achieving subgoals.
        *   **Modularity:** The hierarchical structure naturally leads to modular policies, which are easier to understand and debug.
